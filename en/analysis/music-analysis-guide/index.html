Musical analysis guide

This guide connects representations, audio descriptors, score and video annotation, and analytical uses of iAnalyse. It is neither a treatise on musical acoustics nor a fixed analytical method. Its aim is to show how signal processing and multimodal annotation can support musicological work grounded in listening, observation, comparison and interpretation.

Technical details of each calculation are presented in:

  • Audio descriptors.
  • Spectral representations.

This guide instead starts from the analyst’s questions.

Assisted analysis as a tool​

iAnalyse can be understood as an environment for augmented listening. Visualizations and measurements are not intended to generate a work’s musical meaning automatically. They transform the sound signal into observable objects to support six fundamental operations:

  1. Navigate files, particularly long recordings.
  2. Remember music with overview representations.
  3. Identify clues to investigate through listening and musical analysis.
  4. Extend the analysis: compare, experiment, make observations explicit, communicate, document and connect with musical practice.
  5. Annotate multimedia files, scores, videos and other materials.
  6. Build multimedia journeys through musical analysis.

From signal to interpretation: four distinct levels​

A rigorous approach avoids moving directly from a numerical result to a musicological conclusion. Four levels should be distinguished.

LevelQuestionExample
Computed dataWhat did the algorithm measure?The spectral centroid increases
Observable clueWhich acoustic phenomenon could explain this change?Energy shifts toward higher frequencies
Musical hypothesisWhat function might this phenomenon serve?Brightening timbre, increasing density or changing orchestration
InterpretationHow does this phenomenon act within the work?Preparing a formal articulation or gradually transforming material

An increase in centroid does not by itself mean “tension”, “climax” or “expressive brightness”. These concepts result from a contextual interpretation, confirmed through listening and connected to other parameters.

The recommended path is:

mesure -> observation -> hypothèse -> écoute ciblée -> annotation -> comparaison -> interprétation

Representations and descriptors: complementary approaches​

Representations provide an overview​

A spectrogram, chromagram or self-similarity matrix (SSM) preserves several dimensions at once. These views suit exploration, pattern recognition and memorization.

They address questions such as:

  • where are significant events?
  • which regions resemble one another?
  • how does register or texture evolve?
  • which structures recur?
  • are there continuous processes, breaks or symmetries?

Descriptors isolate a property​

A descriptor reduces each frame to one or a few values. It lets you follow a chosen dimension precisely, compare passages and look for thresholds or trends.

It addresses questions such as:

  • does energy actually increase?
  • does the spectral distribution become higher or broader?
  • does the texture become noisier?
  • does the spectrum change rapidly?
  • does the content become more lateral?

Combining them is more productive than using either alone​

Représentation synoptique -> fait apparaître une forme ou une rupture
Descripteur              -> précise la dimension acoustique concernée
Écoute                   -> identifie le phénomène musical
Annotation               -> inscrit l'interprétation dans la temporalité de l'œuvre

For example, a break visible in a spectrogram can be checked against spectral flux, RMS and centroid. Listening then helps determine whether it is a new source, a change in playing technique, filtering, an edit or a broader formal articulation.

Function 1 — Navigate long audio files​

The challenge​

In a long recording, linear listening alone makes precise returns, comparisons between distant regions and rapid overviews difficult. Representations turn the file into a map of time.

Preferred tools​

Navigation needRepresentation or descriptorClue to look for
Identify silence and large massesWaveform, RMS, LoudnessAlternating activity/rest, overall level changes
Locate attacksWaveform, Onset Strength, Attack TimeImpulsive events and sequence beginnings
Identify texture changesSpectrogram, complexity, Spectral FluxBreaks or transformations in frequency content
Follow register changesLogarithmic spectrogram, Peak Frequency, Spectral CentroidOverall movement toward low or high frequencies
Find harmonic regionsChromagramPitch-class configurations
Identify returnsSSM, chromagram, MFCCDiagonal patterns or similar blocks
Explore spatial evolutionStereo balance, MID/SIDEMovement or width changes

Procedure​

  1. Start with a general view: waveform and spectrogram.
  2. Observe large-scale contrasts without immediately trying to name them.
  3. Place markers at breaks, thresholds, returns and distinctive regions.
  4. Add one or two descriptors corresponding to the main hypothesis.
  5. Listen around the markers, including a few seconds before and after.
  6. Turn relevant markers into segments or annotations.
  7. Return to an overview to check that the segmentation is coherent.

Precaution​

An event’s visibility depends on the settings. An attack may be clear with a short window and disappear with a long one; a low resonance may behave oppositely. Effective navigation may therefore require several maps of the same file.

Function 2 — Support musical memory​

Visualization as external memory​

Listening unfolds over time: what has just been heard must be retained to compare it with what follows. An overview representation makes distant events visible simultaneously. It acts as an external memory of the work.

This is particularly important for:

  • long works;
  • music without a score;
  • experimental works;
  • improvisation;
  • soundscapes and field-recording collections;
  • gradual processes that are difficult to segment;
  • comparisons between versions.

The self-similarity matrix (SSM) as a synopsis​

A self-similarity matrix, or SSM, compares each moment or segment of the file with every other one.

Audio -> extraction de vecteurs par fenêtres
      -> comparaison de chaque vecteur avec tous les autres
      -> matrice temps × temps

The horizontal and vertical axes represent the same file. The main diagonal corresponds to each moment’s identity with itself.

Shape observed in an SSMHypothesis to check
Diagonal line parallel to the main diagonalRepetition or return of a sequence
Homogeneous square along the diagonalRelatively stable section
Checkerboard or repeated blocksPeriodic organization, alternation or recurring form
Sharp break between blocksPossible section boundary
Interrupted or distorted diagonalVaried, accelerated, slowed or transformed repetition

An SSM does not tell you what repeats. Similarity may arise from timbre, energy, harmony or texture, depending on the data used in the comparison.

Choose what the visual memory should retain​

What you want to rememberUseful view
Energy profile and densityWaveform, RMS, Loudness
Registers and spectral morphologiesLinear or logarithmic spectrograms
Harmonic trajectoryChromagram
Timbral familiesMFCC and timbre-based SSM
Returns and overall transformationsSSM
Spatial organizationStereo and MID/SIDE spectrograms

Build an analytical image, rather than merely recognize an image​

Memorization becomes analytical when connected to annotations:

forme visuelle -> écoute du passage -> qualification -> annotation -> relation avec d'autres passages

A color or pattern should not substitute for sound. It serves as a reference for recalling a quality heard, function or hypothesis.

Function 3 — Identify clues to investigate through listening​

What is a clue?​

A clue is an observable fact that may guide analysis without yet constituting proof or a sufficient interpretation.

Examples:

  • simultaneous increases in RMS and centroid;
  • a secondary diagonal in an SSM;
  • a stable horizontal band in a spectrogram;
  • a sudden MFCC change;
  • increased SIDE content;
  • a persistent pitch class in a chromagram.

A clue becomes musically relevant when it is:

  • precisely located;
  • described without overinterpretation;
  • checked by listening;
  • compared with its context;
  • connected to other clues;
  • integrated into a hypothesis about the work.

Triangulation​

A useful practice combines three sources of knowledge:

VISUALISATION : où et sous quelle forme le phénomène apparaît-il ?
MESURE        : quelle dimension varie et dans quelle direction ?
ÉCOUTE        : qu'entend-on, dans quel contexte et avec quelle fonction ?

When they converge, the hypothesis becomes stronger. When they diverge, the divergence is itself informative: it may reveal unsuitable settings, a perceptual limitation or a separation between acoustic dimensions.

Further functions of assisted analysis​

The three main functions—navigation, memory and identifying clues—extend into several important uses in digital musicology.

Function 4 — Compare​

Aligning representations and using common measurements facilitates comparisons between:

  • distant moments in a work;
  • different occurrences of material;
  • performances;
  • versions, edits or restorations;
  • works in the same corpus;
  • different musical or sonic practices.

The computer helps make comparison criteria explicit. A resemblance may concern energy, timbre, register, harmony, morphology or space without being an overall similarity.

Ressemblance perçue
    -> choix d'une dimension à comparer
    -> représentation ou descripteur commun
    -> contrôle des paramètres et des sources
    -> écoute comparative
    -> qualification des invariants et des différences

This moves beyond “these passages resemble each other” to ask precisely how, at what scale, and despite which differences.

Function 5 — Experiment and discover​

Visualizations can serve a heuristic function: they sometimes reveal a phenomenon that was not being sought.

Examples:

  • spectral regularity unnoticed during initial listening;
  • a transformed return suggested by an SSM diagonal;
  • spatial organization connected to form;
  • a separation between dynamics and brightness;
  • timbral continuity across supposedly distinct sections.

Exploration is productive when graphical discovery is distinguished from musical relevance. An unexpected shape becomes a research lead, not a conclusion.

A heuristic approach may alternate between:

question initiale
    -> exploration visuelle
    -> anomalie ou motif inattendu
    -> nouvelle question
    -> écoute ciblée
    -> choix d'une mesure plus spécifique
    -> reformulation de l'hypothèse

Function 6 — Make observations explicit and reproducible​

Making observations explicit does not mean eliminating subjectivity. It means stating the operations that lead to an observation:

  • audio source used;
  • passage boundaries;
  • channel or channel mix;
  • window and hop settings;
  • chosen representation and descriptors;
  • thresholds, bounds and normalizations;
  • segmentation or comparison criteria.

This information allows another person to reproduce the calculation, discuss the choices and obtain a comparable visualization. It distinguishes computed results, perceptual description and interpretation.

Reproducibility strengthens an argument without making the interpretation unique. Two analysts may share observations and propose different musical readings.

Function 7 — Communicate and teach​

Overview images, curves and annotations can mediate between the work and different audiences:

  • guide classroom listening;
  • explain an acoustic phenomenon;
  • present the form of a work without a score;
  • connect perceptual vocabulary with signal data;
  • communicate an analytical journey;
  • document a performance or practice-based research.

A teaching visualization should be more selective than an exploratory interface. It benefits from few parameters, a clear legend and synchronization with audible examples.

The recommended teaching sequence is:

écouter sans image
    -> verbaliser les perceptions
    -> observer une représentation ciblée
    -> relier forme visible et phénomène acoustique
    -> réécouter
    -> discuter ce que l'image explique et ce qu'elle ne montre pas

This alternation avoids letting the image supply the answer in advance or impose a single way of hearing.

Function 8 — Document and argue​

iAnalyse preserves stages of reasoning within one temporal workspace:

  • navigation markers;
  • proposed segmentation;
  • descriptive and interpretive annotations;
  • relationships between events;
  • representations that prompted the hypothesis;
  • curves used as evidence;
  • reservations and competing hypotheses.

This turns analysis into an inspectable, revisable record. It supports scholarly writing, presentation preparation, collaborative work and analytical editions.

A strong argument does more than insert a spectrogram screenshot. It explains what to observe, why the settings are appropriate, how the phenomenon is perceived and how it supports the interpretation.

Function 9 — Connect analysis and musical practice​

The same tools can support composition, performance and sound production:

  • compare takes;
  • observe the spectral balance of instrumental playing;
  • study a gesture’s evolution;
  • prepare a performance from articulation points;
  • analyze spatialization or a mix;
  • retrieve and transform material during creative work.

Here, computed results can provide feedback: they not only describe a finished work but inform artistic decisions. Measurement remains one perspective among others and does not define an aesthetic standard.

Multimodal analysis​

iAnalyse goes beyond examining the audio signal. Slideshow and Video Documents let you place annotations directly on a score, image or film shot while connecting them with the media’s temporal progression.

This changes the status of annotation:

annotation temporelle
    = à quel moment le phénomène se produit-il ?

annotation spatiale
    = où se trouve-t-il dans la partition ou dans l'image ?

annotation audiovisuelle
    = comment sa position, sa durée et son contenu visuel
      se rapportent-ils à ce qui est entendu ?

Multimodal analysis can connect several spaces:

  • recording time;
  • the score’s graphical space;
  • the video frame;
  • editing time;
  • computed sound representations;
  • the conceptual space built by analytical categories and relationships.

Function 10 — Annotate a score in a Slideshow Document​

Principle​

A Slideshow Document connects a sequence of slides with media time. Each slide may contain a score image and its own annotations. Playback position determines the displayed page and the objects’ temporal visibility.

Audio ou vidéo
    -> positions temporelles des changements de page
    -> diapositives contenant les images de partition
    -> annotations spatiales sur chaque page
    -> durée d'apparition des annotations
    -> lecture synchronisée partition / média / commentaire analytique

Image zoom and alignment adapt the score to the presentation frame. Annotations remain organized by slide and also appear in the Document’s timeline.

What score annotation enables​

OperationAnalytical example
LocateFrame a cell, note, chord, motif or indication
ConnectShow correspondences between voices, motifs, registers or pages
Establish hierarchyDistinguish main structure, detail and secondary commentary
Place in timeReveal an annotation when the passage is heard
CompareUse consistent colors or categories across occurrences
CommentAssociate text, a symbol or an explanation with a precise region
GuideBuild a reading path that follows the performance

Graphical shapes, lines, text and composite annotations can distinguish levels of discourse: description, segmentation, motivic relationships, harmonic function, orchestration or interpretive hypotheses.

Spatial and temporal annotation​

A score annotation has two complementary anchors:

  • a graphical position on the page;
  • a time interval in the recording.

This dual placement distinguishes:

  • the written object, which may occupy a stable region of the score;
  • its sonic realization, which has a duration and a place within the performance;
  • the analytical commentary, which may appear before, during or after the event.

For a repeat, cadenza, rubato or tempo change, the relationship between notated space and recorded time is nonlinear. Slide changes and annotation durations can document this difference.

Uses in musical analysis​

Motivic and thematic analysis​

  • outline occurrences of a motif;
  • use a consistent color for its identity;
  • add a second graphical property for its transformation;
  • connect antecedents and consequents;
  • compare augmentation, diminution, inversion, fragmentation or transposition.

Color then represents an analytical category, not a computed acoustic property. Its legend should be explicit.

Harmonic and contrapuntal analysis​

  • annotate chords, functions, voice leading or prolongations;
  • show common tones and chromatic movement;
  • distinguish local and global harmonic levels;
  • follow a pedal point or structural line across pages;
  • reveal analyses during listening.

Orchestration analysis​

  • color instrumental families or orchestral functions;
  • identify doubling, handovers, timbral fusion and contrast;
  • connect notation with the spectrogram and descriptors;
  • compare notated density, sonic density and recorded balance.

Performance analysis​

  • align the score with a specific recording;
  • annotate breathing, rubato, accentuation, phrasing and articulation;
  • compare notated and performed duration;
  • identify choices of balance, dynamics or timbre;
  • prepare comparisons between performances.

A crescendo marking in the score, an increase in RMS and perceived intensification are three different objects. Comparing them is precisely what makes it possible to study their realization in performance.

Contemporary music and unconventional notation​

  • comment on symbols, playing techniques and setups;
  • explain open or mobile sections;
  • follow the relationship between prescriptive notation and sonic result;
  • annotate graphic scores;
  • document performers’ decisions.

Communication, teaching and outreach​

The Slideshow can become an augmented listening score:

page de partition
    + surlignage synchronisé
    + commentaire progressif
    + écoute
    + possibilité de revenir au contexte

Uses include:

  • analysis classes with progressively revealed levels;
  • presenting a motif as it enters;
  • synchronized orchestration commentary;
  • introductions to score reading;
  • lectures or concerts with commentary;
  • performer preparation;
  • introducing a contemporary work;
  • publishing an accessible analytical journey.

Effective outreach avoids showing every annotation at once. It organizes a progression: identification, listening, explanation, comparison and synthesis.

Methodological risks​

  • The page may suggest that the score is the entire work, relegating performance to the background.
  • Highlighting directs attention toward the analyst’s prior choices.
  • A page change is not necessarily a musical articulation.
  • Synchronization with one recording does not apply to every performance.
  • An annotation placed on a note may concern a phenomenon extending beyond its notation.

It is therefore useful to distinguish visually between information from the score, listening, computation and interpretation.

Analyze music for moving images​

Analyzing music for moving images involves more than listing synchronizations. It examines relationships between two temporal and expressive organizations.

Synchronization​

  • coincidence between a sound attack and a cut;
  • musical accent on a movement;
  • harmonic change at a shot transition;
  • appearance or disappearance of a sound with a visible source;
  • exact, anticipated, delayed or gradually established synchronization.

A synchronization point becomes analytically interesting when its preparation, recurrence and function are also examined.

Editing and form​

Visual dimensionMusical dimensions to compare
Shot durationPhrases, pulse, event density
CutAttack, silence, cadence, spectral break
FadeTransition, timbral continuity, resonance
Alternating shotsOstinato, periodicity, dialogue, antiphony
Long takeContinuous process, sustained sound, accumulation
Change of shot scaleRegister, density, dynamics, orchestration

The aim is not to reduce the relationship to one-to-one correspondences, but to observe agreement, displacement, tension and independence between editing and music.

Point of view, space and listening​

  • relationship between a source’s position in the frame and its stereo position;
  • off-screen or acousmatic sounds, or sounds with ambiguous sources;
  • changes in listening perspective;
  • sonic continuity across visual discontinuity;
  • diegetic space, off-screen space or musical commentary;
  • depth suggested by reverberation, level or filtering.

The stereo balance spectrogram and MID/SIDE ratio can complement frame annotation, but do not alone determine a sound’s narrative or spatial status.

Dramaturgical and narrative functions​

  • continuity or rupture between sequences;
  • anticipation of a visual event;
  • recall or memory association;
  • characterization of a place, character or state;
  • change of narrative level;
  • irony, contradiction or distance between music and image.

These functions cannot be computed directly. Measurements help describe the material that realizes them, while audiovisual annotation supports arguments about their contextual role.

Analyze gesture and performance​

Video annotation also opens up the study of music as bodily activity.

Instrumental gesture​

  • preparation, attack, sustain and release;
  • coordination between hands;
  • sound-producing and expressive gestures;
  • relationship between movement amplitude and acoustic result;
  • bodily anticipation of a sound event;
  • playing techniques and technical changes.

Interaction​

  • looks and signals between musicians;
  • conductor–ensemble coordination;
  • imitation and response;
  • turn-taking in improvisation;
  • temporary leadership;
  • relationships between collective movement and musical density.

Stage and setup​

  • performers’ movement;
  • use of interfaces and electronics;
  • relationship with the audience;
  • visible spatial arrangement and heard spatialization;
  • staging, lighting and sound production.

Object tracking can keep a graphical reference on a moving element. It does not replace gesture analysis: a geometric trajectory alone does not define musical function.

Analyze dance and movement​

A video can connect:

  • bodily weight shifts and accents;
  • choreographic and musical phrases;
  • synchronization, counterpoint or independence;
  • movement density and sonic density;
  • spatial occupation and spatialization;
  • repeated gestures and musical recurrences.

Annotations can follow bodily regions or events, while markers and segments organize the temporal units being compared.

Analyze recorded practices​

Beyond film and concert, video annotation is relevant to:

  • audiovisual ethnomusicology;
  • rehearsal observation;
  • analysis of lessons and oral transmission;
  • instrument making and technical gestures;
  • sound installations;
  • interactive setups;
  • studio creation;
  • practice-based research documentation.

In these contexts, account for recording conditions, camera angle, what remains off-screen, and the effect of the recording equipment’s presence.

Methodological risks​

  • Temporal coincidence does not prove causation.
  • What is visible is not necessarily the source of what is heard.
  • Editing may reconstruct the performance.
  • Framing excludes part of the situation.
  • Automatic tracking may lose the object or drift.
  • An annotation fixed to the frame and one attached to an object do not have the same meaning.
  • Gesture or narrative-function categories must be defined before quantitative comparison.

Function 12 — Build multimedia analytical journeys​

Slideshow and Video Documents can move beyond individual note-taking to become forms of publication or presentation.

Guided listening journeys​

A journey may organize:

  1. an initial listening without commentary;
  2. an overview of form;
  3. several stages focused on specific phenomena;
  4. comparative returns;
  5. a final integrative listening.

Time-based annotations control what appears at each stage. The journey can respect the time needed for discovery rather than immediately presenting a finished analysis.

Synchronized analytical edition​

An edition may combine:

  • facsimile or score;
  • recording;
  • graphical annotations;
  • textual comments;
  • segmentation;
  • acoustic representations;
  • links between occurrences;
  • performance variants.

It preserves the distinction between source, observation and commentary while enabling coordinated consultation.

Teaching and self-study materials​

One Document may offer several layers:

  • basic orientation for a first approach;
  • vocabulary and definitions;
  • detailed analysis;
  • questions to solve by listening;
  • delayed display of answers;
  • comparisons between students’ proposals.

Scholarly communication and public outreach​

In a presentation, synchronized annotations show exactly the passage under discussion. For public outreach, they can make a structure perceptible without requiring prior score reading.

In both cases, the visualization should remain readable and its origin explicit: computed data, transcription, analyst’s annotation or source information.

Objective — Study gesture, performance and interaction​

Questions​

  • Which gesture prepares or produces the sound event?
  • Which visual clues coordinate performers?
  • How do body, instrument and space contribute to form?
  • What does video show that audio alone cannot establish?

Recommended combination​

  • annotations or tracking on relevant bodily and instrumental regions;
  • time intervals for gesture phases;
  • waveform and Onset Strength for attacks;
  • spectrogram for sonic results;
  • segments for interaction units;
  • explicit categories to make observations comparable.

Do not confuse visible gesture with acoustic cause. A gesture may prepare, accompany, symbolize or follow a sound without directly producing it.

Objective — Segment and study form​

Musicological questions​

  • Where are the articulations?
  • Are there stable sections, transitions or continuous processes?
  • Are boundaries prepared, abrupt or ambiguous?
  • Which parameters change together at each articulation?

Recommended combination​

RoleTools
OverviewWaveform, spectrogram, SSM
Change detectionSpectral Flux, Onset Strength, spectral complexity
Energy confirmationRMS, Loudness
Timbral confirmationSpectral Centroid, Flatness, MFCC
FormalizationMarkers, segments, annotations and relationships

Analytical pipeline​

Vue globale
    -> repérage de frontières candidates
    -> calcul de flux, énergie et timbre
    -> écoute avant / pendant / après chaque frontière
    -> distinction rupture / transition / simple événement local
    -> annotation de la fonction formelle

A spectral-flux peak may signal an articulation or simply an isolated attack within a section. Formal function depends on temporal scale and what happens after the peak.

Objective — Study timbre, texture and playing techniques​

Musicological questions​

  • Is the material tonal, noisy, harmonic or inharmonic?
  • How do brightness, density and spectral width evolve?
  • Can families of sounds or playing techniques be distinguished?
  • Is transformation continuous or based on substitutions?

Recommended combination​

DimensionRepresentationComplementary descriptors
BrightnessSpectrogramSpectral Centroid, Rolloff, Sharpness, Slope
Tonality/noiseSpectrogram, logarithmic resolutionFlatness, Noisiness, Harmonic Ratio, Inharmonicity
Width and dispersionSpectrogramSpectral Variance, Standard Deviation
Envelope regularityMFCCSpectral Smoothness, Contrast, Crest
Temporal transformationComplexity, MFCCSpectral Flux, Reflux, Entropy

Method​

  1. First describe visible morphology: lines, clouds, bands, impacts, halos.
  2. Identify dimensions that seem to vary.
  3. Add only descriptors corresponding to those dimensions.
  4. Listen to several presumed occurrences of the same family.
  5. Annotate similarities and internal differences.
  6. Check whether the categories remain relevant in other contexts.

MFCCs are useful for finding related timbres, but less explicit for naming them. The spectrogram and listening remain necessary to understand what produces the similarity.

Objective — Study pitch, register and harmony​

Musicological questions​

  • Which pitches or register regions structure the passage?
  • Are there pedal points, pitch centers, aggregates or glissandi?
  • How do chords and pitch classes succeed one another?
  • Do harmonic timbre and perceived pitch converge?

Recommended combination​

NeedTools
Read partials and possible fundamentalsLogarithmic spectrogram or logarithmic resolution
Follow a dominant frequencyPeak Frequency
Follow an overall register shiftSpectral Centroid and Rolloff
Observe pitch classesChromagram
Assess the spectrum’s harmonic organizationHarmonic Ratio, Inharmonicity

Precautions​

  • The spectral peak is not necessarily the fundamental.
  • Centroid describes an energy center, not a musical pitch.
  • The chromagram merges octaves and aggregates harmonics.
  • An inharmonic sound may produce visually strong pitch classes.
  • The reference tuning must match the recording.

The best approach often combines logarithmic spectrogram and chromagram: the former preserves register and partials, while the latter summarizes pitch classes.

Objective — Study dynamics, energy and density​

Musicological questions​

  • Where are accumulations, thinning, crescendos and decays?
  • Do level changes coincide with timbral changes?
  • Does a perceived climax arise from amplitude, register, density or their combination?

Example of reasoning​

Impression de crescendo
    -> RMS augmente-t-il ?
    -> le spectre s'élargit-il ?
    -> le centroïde monte-t-il ?
    -> les attaques deviennent-elles plus nombreuses ?
    -> l'écoute confirme-t-elle une intensification ou seulement un éclaircissement ?

Several acoustic trajectories can produce perceived intensification. Describing them separately avoids reducing musical dynamics to sound level alone.

Objective — Study rhythm, attacks and activity​

Musicological questions​

  • Where do events concentrate?
  • Do attacks form a pulse, gesture or texture?
  • Does activity become more regular, dense or discontinuous?

Tools​

  • waveform for visible impacts;
  • Onset Strength for the prominence of event beginnings;
  • Attack Time for their morphology;
  • Spectral Flux for changes between frames;
  • complexity in increase mode to locate emerging bands;
  • Zero Crossing Rate for some noisy or highly active textures.

An onset detector does not automatically provide musical rhythm. Peaks must be grouped, hierarchized and interpreted according to tempo, any meter, accentuation and texture.

Objective — Study stereo space​

Musicological questions​

  • How are events distributed between left, center and right?
  • Does width vary with register?
  • Which materials belong to the center and which to the lateral environment?
  • Does spatialization contribute to form?

Recommended combination​

DimensionRepresentationDescriptor
Left/right position by frequencyStereo balance spectrogramStereo Balance
Center versus widthMID/SIDE spectrogramMID/SIDE Ratio
Overall evolutionCurves of both descriptorsComparison with formal segments

Representations show where the phenomenon lies spectrally; descriptors summarize its overall trend. Listening on headphones and loudspeakers remains essential.

High SIDE content is not necessarily a “sound far to the side”. It may result from reverberation, delay, phase opposition or different channel content.

Objective — Identify repetitions, transformations and families​

Musicological questions​

  • Does material return unchanged or transformed?
  • What remains invariant: timbre, rhythm, harmony, contour or energy?
  • Can events be grouped into families?

Tools​

Type of resemblancePreferred tools
TimbralMFCC, spectrogram, SSM
HarmonicChromagram, pitch-based SSM
Energy-relatedRMS, Loudness
Overall spectralCentroid, Flatness, Rolloff, Entropy
MorphologicalAnnotations, visual spectrogram comparison

Approach​

  1. Use the SSM or an overview to detect a potential resemblance.
  2. Isolate the passages and listen to them consecutively.
  3. Describe what seems invariant and what varies.
  4. Choose descriptors corresponding to those dimensions.
  5. Annotate the relationship: repetition, variation, derivation, contrast or simple acoustic resemblance.

Acoustic similarity does not imply a common origin or function. Two events can resemble one another without belonging to the same musical material.

Objective — Compare versions, performances or works​

Comparison conditions​

To make curves and images comparable, retain:

  • the same sample rate;
  • the same window sizes and types;
  • the same hop;
  • the same frequency bounds;
  • the same thresholds and contrasts;
  • the same palettes and display dynamics;
  • the same channel selection.

What can vary independently of performance​

  • recording technique and microphone placement;
  • venue acoustics;
  • mixing and mastering;
  • background noise;
  • dynamic compression;
  • tuning and reference pitch;
  • recording speed or editing.

Document these factors. A centroid difference between two performances may arise from playing, but also from microphones or equalization.

Alignment​

When durations differ, compare corresponding events or segments rather than absolute times. Annotations then provide a common musicological layer across different temporalities.

Three temporal scales​

A work often needs to be observed at several scales.

ScaleObjectsTools
MicroAttack, grain, partial, brief transitionShort window, waveform, spectrogram, Onset

A strong micro-scale boundary is not necessarily important at the formal scale. Conversely, a gradual formal transition may produce no striking local peak.

A general protocol in iAnalyse​

1. Prepare​

  • identify the source, version and recording conditions;
  • note duration, channels, any reference tuning and file-specific features;
  • formulate an initial musical question.

2. Map​

  • display waveform and spectrogram;
  • browse the file quickly;
  • place provisional markers;
  • produce an SSM if duration or form justifies it.

3. Listen​

  • listen to identified regions in context;
  • describe what is heard before assigning a function;
  • adjust markers and create segments.

4. Measure​

  • choose a small number of descriptors directly related to the question;
  • keep parameters consistent;
  • identify convergence, divergence and outliers.

5. Interpret​

  • turn observations into hypotheses;
  • associate each hypothesis with supporting and contrary evidence;
  • compare occurrences and exceptions.

6. Document​

  • record results in annotations;
  • distinguish description, hypothesis and interpretation;
  • connect the relevant events or segments;
  • retain settings needed for reproducibility.

7. Revise​

  • return to listening without looking at the curves;
  • check that the interpretation remains audible and relevant;
  • actively seek passages that contradict it;
  • reformulate the hypothesis if necessary.

The role of annotation​

Annotation is where computed data becomes a musicological object. It may contain:

  • the phenomenon’s location;
  • a perceptual description;
  • visual and numerical clues;
  • the analytical hypothesis;
  • degree of certainty;
  • relationships with other events;
  • settings used;
  • objections or alternative interpretations.

A useful formulation explicitly separates levels:

Observation: simultaneous increases in RMS, centroid and spectral flux between 12:40 and 12:52.
Listening: accumulation of layers, gradual opening of the high register and accelerating attacks.
Hypothesis: an intensification process preparing the articulation at 12:53.
Reservation: the centroid increase may be amplified by the entry of a single high source.

Epistemological limitations​

Computation depends on choices​

Window, hop, threshold, normalization, bounds and palette construct the observed object. A visualization is a technical interpretation of the signal before it is interpreted musically.

What is measurable does not encompass everything audible​

Available descriptors emphasize certain dimensions: energy, spectral distribution, change, harmony or spatiality. They represent phrasing, intention, function, syntax, perceived causality, gesture and cultural meaning less directly.

The visible can dominate listening​

A salient graphical shape attracts attention and may lead to overestimating a perceptually minor phenomenon. Alternating listening with and without the display is useful.

Repeatability is not neutrality​

A reproducible calculation may rely on a model unsuited to the material. Numerical stability guarantees that the same procedure produces the same result, not that this result is the best musical description.

Correlation is not function​

Two parameters may evolve together without sharing a cause or function. Their relationship must be examined in the work’s context.

Good wording practices​

Prefer:

  • “the centroid increases” to “the sound becomes tense”;
  • “a similarity appears in the SSM” to “the theme returns”;
  • “pitch class D is dominant” to “the chord is D major”;
  • “the SIDE ratio increases” to “the source moves sideways”;
  • “flux indicates a spectral change” to “a new section begins”.

Interpretive wording may follow, supported by arguments that justify moving from one level to another.

Example of an integrated analytical journey​

Question​

How does a long electroacoustic work build its major articulations through timbral transformations?

Journey​

1. Forme d'onde + spectrogramme
   -> repérage des grandes masses et silences

2. SSM
   -> recherche de zones stables, retours et transitions

3. Marqueurs provisoires
   -> localisation des frontières candidates

4. Flux spectral + RMS + centroïde + flatness
   -> distinction entre changement d'énergie, de registre et de texture

5. MFCC et spectrogramme de complexité
   -> observation des transformations timbrales internes

6. Écoute ciblée
   -> qualification des gestes, matières, continuités et ruptures

7. Segmentation et annotations
   -> formulation d'une hypothèse formelle

8. Retour à l'écoute intégrale
   -> vérification de la pertinence perceptive de la segmentation

Expected result​

The result is not a segmentation “discovered” by the algorithm, but an analytical proposal supported by computed, visual and auditory evidence.

Conclusion​

From a digital musicology perspective, iAnalyse does more than produce images or curves. It organizes an inquiry into sound:

naviguer pour trouver
mémoriser pour comparer
mesurer pour préciser
écouter pour comprendre
annoter pour argumenter

Representations provide access to overall forms, descriptors isolate acoustic dimensions, and annotations build the bridge to musical analysis. Slideshow Documents place this reasoning on the score; Video Documents extend it to framing, editing, gesture and performance.

Their combination supports a practice in which the computer does not conclude on the analyst’s behalf, but helps them see, hear, retrieve, compare, communicate and formulate.
