Audio representations

This page describes the spectral representations computed by iAnalyse: linear and logarithmic spectrograms, chromagram, MFCC, spectral complexity spectrogram, logarithmic-resolution representation (“wavelet”), and the two stereo spatial spectrograms.

The aim is to explain the actual pipeline, how parameters affect the image, and musical uses, rather than to derive every formula. A representation is never a neutral transcription of sound: window, hop, scale and dynamic-range settings emphasize some phenomena while hiding others.

Reading the images​

In every time-based image:

  • the horizontal axis represents time;
  • the vertical axis represents frequency, pitch, pitch class or coefficient index, depending on the representation;
  • color or brightness generally represents normalized energy;
  • each image column corresponds to an analysis frame, not a durationless instant.

The common pipeline is:

Fichier audio
    -> décodage et conversion du signal
    -> sélection ou mélange des canaux
    -> découpage en fenêtres recouvrantes
    -> multiplication par une fenêtre d'analyse
    -> FFT à 44 100 Hz
    -> filtrage fréquentiel éventuel
    -> transformation propre à la représentation
    -> normalisation et conversion en image
    -> palette, dynamique et transparence d'affichage

Computation and display are separate stages. Changing the palette or display dynamics does not necessarily recompute the analysis; changing window size, hop, spectral type or its parameters produces new data.

Parameters shared by all spectral representations​

Channel​

For a multichannel source, iAnalyse lets you choose a specific channel or a mix of the channels. Spatial spectrograms always use the first two channels.

SettingEffect on the visualizationMusical uses and precautions
MixCombines channels into a mono signalConvenient overview, but components in phase opposition may be attenuated
Isolated channelDisplays only one channelUseful for comparing a recording, track or side of the stereo field
Two spatial channelsKeeps left and right separateRequired to analyze panning, width and decorrelation

FFT window size​

Window size determines the fundamental trade-off between temporal and frequency precision.

Petite fenêtre -> attaques nettes dans le temps -> fréquences plus épaisses et moins précises
Grande fenêtre -> fréquences et partiels mieux séparés -> attaques étalées dans le temps

At 44,100 Hz, a window of 2,048 samples lasts about 46 ms, and the FFT bin spacing is about 21.5 Hz.

ChoiceVisual appearanceMusical uses favored
Small windowSharp transients, broad frequency bandsPercussion, articulation, fast rhythms, segmentation
Medium windowReadable compromiseGeneral analysis, timbre, mixed textures
Large windowFine harmonic lines, blurred event timingSustained pitches, beating, neighboring partials, low resonances

A very large window does not create information: it improves frequency separation by averaging over a longer duration.

Window hop​

In iAnalyse, the effective hop is proportional to window size:

pas = taille de fenêtre × réglage du pas / 10

The default setting of 7 therefore advances by 70% of the window, giving 30% overlap.

HopVisual appearanceAnalytical consequence
SmallMany columns, smoother transitionsBetter temporal tracking, greater computation and storage needs
LargeMore discontinuous or coarse imageFast computation, but brief events may fall between frames

The hop changes the analysis’s temporal sampling, not the intrinsic frequency resolution of each FFT.

Window type​

The analysis window attenuates the edges of each frame to limit spectral leakage.

Window familyExpected appearanceSuggested use
RectangularNarrow peaks but strong leakage around frequencies not aligned with binsSpecial measurements on very stable signals
Hann/HanningGood compromise, smooth appearanceRecommended general choice
HammingControlled sidelobes, resolution close to HannGeneral analysis of tonal signals
Blackman and strongly tapered windowsCleaner background around strong peaks, broader peaksFinding weak components near strong ones

The exact names of the available windows depend on the application’s menu. Their common role is to set the balance between peak sharpness and spectral leakage.

Dynamic range in dB​

Dynamic range determines the gap between the strongest components and the lowest level retained in the image.

ValueVisual effectMusical use
Short range, for example −40 dBHigh contrast; only strong components remain visibleFundamentals, dominant attacks, clear structural reading
Medium range, for example −60 to −80 dBBalance between detail and readabilityTimbre, resonances, general analysis
Long range, for example −100 dBMany weak details and more noiseDecays, reverberation, hiss, secondary components

An extended range can give background noise excessive visual prominence. Do not confuse it with the work’s overall musical dynamics.

Frequency filters​

Optional filters zero out regions of the spectrum before representation-specific transformations.

Note the current internal terminology: activateLowPass removes the lower region up to the chosen frequency, while activateHighPass removes the upper region from the chosen frequency onward. In the interface, check the visual effect rather than relying solely on the field’s technical name.

SettingVisual effectMusical use
Remove low frequenciesRemoves DC, hum and low-frequency energyExamine attacks, noise and the high register
Remove high frequenciesRemoves hiss and high componentsExamine fundamentals, bass and dark masses

Filtering affects the analysis: removed content no longer contributes to subsequent calculations, including chromagrams, MFCCs and spatial representations.

Palette, display dynamics and transparency​

The palette maps numerical values to colors. It adds no acoustic information.

Display parameterEffectRecommendation
Multicolor paletteVisually separates several levelsUseful for exploring energy in detail, but may suggest false categorical thresholds
Two-color or monochrome paletteMore restrained intensity displayUseful for comparing representations or printing
Display dynamicsLightens or darkens already computed dataAdjust without confusing this with FFT dynamic range in dB
Transparent backgroundMakes overlays easierCheck contrast against annotations and other layers

Keep the same palette and settings when comparing excerpts. Different palettes can make two spectra look more different than they are.

Linear spectrogram​

Principle and pipeline​

The linear spectrogram stacks successive FFT magnitudes on a vertical axis uniformly graduated in hertz.

Signal
    -> fenêtres recouvrantes
    -> fenêtre d'analyse
    -> FFT et conversion en dynamique logarithmique
    -> filtres éventuels
    -> réduction verticale pour l'image
    -> spectrogramme temps × fréquence linéaire

Equal pixel distances correspond to equal intervals in hertz. A 1,000 Hz band therefore occupies the same height in the low and high registers.

Specific parameters​

This type has no additional spectral parameter: its behavior mainly depends on window size, hop, window type, dynamic range, channel and filters.

What you see​

  • horizontal lines: stable partials or resonances;
  • vertical lines: attacks and impulsive events;
  • broad clouds: noise or wideband texture;
  • curves: glissandi, pitch drift or filter sweeps;
  • halos after events: reverberation or resonance.

Musical uses​

The linear spectrogram is especially suited to analyzing timbre, spectral orchestration, filtering and high-frequency content. It shows the physical spacing of partials clearly and supports measurements in hertz.

It is less convenient for following musical pitches in the bass: low octaves occupy very little height, while high frequencies take up much of the image.

Logarithmic spectrogram​

Principle and pipeline​

The logarithmic spectrogram starts from the FFT and redistributes bins along an axis close to musical pitch: each octave occupies a comparable height.

Signal -> fenêtres -> FFT -> contraste éventuel
       -> projection fréquence vers hauteur logarithmique
       -> seuil d'énergie
       -> spectrogramme temps × hauteur

This projection makes intervals, registers and harmonic structures easier to read.

Specific parameters​

ParameterEffect on the imageMusical use
Contrast: none, medium, high, maximumProgressively emphasizes maxima and attenuates intermediate valuesSeparates harmonic lines; strong contrast may erase noise, reverberation and weak partials
A frequency (aFreq, default 440 Hz)Shifts pitch-grid alignmentAdapt the analysis to historical tuning, a different overall tuning or an out-of-tune source
Threshold (threshold, default 0.4)Removes components below a relative levelCleans the image; an excessive value creates a false impression of a sparse spectrum or silence
Minimum frequency/pitchCrops the bottom and expands the remaining registerExclude DC and sub-bass; focus on the instrumental register
Maximum frequency/pitchCrops the top and expands the remaining registerFocus on useful fundamentals and harmonics, or include high-frequency noise

The note and octave fields describe musical bounds; the lower and upper frequencies are their numerical equivalents used in computation.

Musical uses​

This view is recommended for following melodies, chords, registers, glissandi and harmonic series over several octaves. Transposed patterns retain a comparable vertical geometry.

It is not note detection: each harmonic remains visible and may be mistaken for a fundamental. Precision in the bass still depends on FFT window size.

Chromagram​

Principle and pipeline​

The chromagram folds all pitches separated by octaves into twelve pitch classes. All Cs, for example, contribute to the same row.

Signal -> fenêtres -> FFT -> contraste
       -> conversion des fréquences en hauteurs
       -> regroupement par classe modulo l'octave
       -> seuil et modulation éventuelle par l'énergie RMS
       -> image temps × 12 classes de hauteur

Specific parameters​

ParameterEffect on the imageMusical use
ContrastMakes dominant classes more distinctClarifies chords and tonalities; may hide passing notes and harmonic enrichments
A frequencyAligns classes with the actual tuning referenceEssential for archives, historical instruments or nonstandard tuning
ThresholdRemoves weak contributionsStabilizes chords; too high, it removes weak thirds, extensions and resonances
Low frequency (lowFreq, default 15 Hz)Excludes very low frequencies from foldingPrevents DC, impacts and rumble from contaminating classes
High frequency (highFreq, default 14,080 Hz)Limits the upper harmonics includedReduces noise and cymbals; too low a limit removes useful harmonic evidence
Energy modulationWeights classes by the frame’s relative RMSPreserves dynamics and distinguishes weak/strong regions; when disabled, harmonic reading is less level-dependent

Modulation uses the file’s maximum RMS as its reference. An exceptionally loud event may therefore make the rest of the piece appear visually weaker.

Musical uses​

The chromagram is useful for analyzing harmony, chord progressions, pitch centers, tonality, ostinatos and repetitions transposed by octaves. It is robust to register changes.

It deliberately discards octave, register, partial order and much timbral information. It works less well with noise, untuned percussion, dense multiphonics and inharmonic sounds. A strong class does not prove that a note of that name was played: it may result from several harmonics.

MFCC — Mel Frequency Cepstral Coefficients​

Principle and pipeline​

MFCCs summarize the perceptual spectral envelope. They describe overall timbral shape more than individual frequencies.

Signal -> fenêtres -> FFT
       -> banc de filtres triangulaires sur l'échelle Mel
       -> énergie de chaque bande
       -> logarithme
       -> transformée en cosinus discrète (DCT-II)
       -> 48 coefficients
       -> remplacement des valeurs invalides et normalisation min-max par fenêtre
       -> image temps × indice de coefficient

The vertical axis is neither frequency nor pitch. Low-order coefficients describe broad envelope shapes; higher-order coefficients describe finer details and are often less stable.

Specific parameters​

ParameterEffect on the imageMusical use
Minimum frequencyRemoves the influence of low bands below the boundAvoid rumble and DC, or isolate an instrument’s useful range
Maximum frequencyRemoves bands above the boundReduce hiss/cymbals or target an instrumental family
Number of Mel bands (numMelBands, default 18)Changes the detail of the spectral summary before the DCTFew bands: stable overall shape; more bands: finer timbral details but greater sensitivity to noise and FFT settings

The current implementation produces 48 coefficients. The number of Mel bands controls the input filter bank, not directly the number of final rows.

Impact of common parameters​

Window size is crucial: a small window follows articulation changes quickly, while a large window stabilizes timbral color. Since each frame is min-max normalized, the view mainly highlights the internal coefficient shape rather than absolute level differences over time.

Musical uses​

MFCCs suit timbre comparison, detection of changes in instrumentation, playing techniques, phonemes and textures, and automatic segmentation. Similar visual patterns indicate similar spectral envelopes.

They are less suitable for directly reading notes or partials. An individual coefficient is difficult to interpret musically: the main value lies in the vector’s shape and evolution. Use exactly the same parameters when comparing files.

Spectral complexity spectrogram​

Principle and pipeline​

Despite its internal English name, “Complex Spectrogram”, this representation does not display the complex part of the FFT. It represents the difference between two consecutive spectra.

Fenêtre précédente -> FFT -> magnitudes A
Fenêtre courante   -> FFT -> magnitudes B
                              |
                              -> différence bin par bin
                              -> sélection du mode
                              -> normalisation min-max
                              -> image temps × fréquence

Specific parameter: mode​

ModeValues retainedAppearance and musical use
AllIncreases and decreasesOverall view of spectral change; useful for transitions and texture boundaries
IncreaseEmerging or growing energyAttacks, instrumental entries, filter opening, new bands
DecreaseDisappearing or declining energyDecays, releases, filter closing, resonance endings

Impact of hop and window size​

A small hop compares nearby moments: differences are finer and more continuous. A large hop emphasizes changes but mixes several events. A small window favors transients; a large window makes slow spectral changes more readable.

Musical uses​

This representation is very useful for segmentation, studying attacks, texture transitions, articulation and gradual transformations. It answers “where and in which bands does the spectrum change?” rather than “which frequencies are present?”.

Per-frame normalization can make a small change as bright as a large one. Compare the geometry and location of changes; do not interpret color as an absolute quantity across columns.

Logarithmic-resolution representation (“Wavelet Spectrogram”)​

Principle and terminology​

This representation applies a bank of bands evenly spaced in octaves to FFT magnitudes. Two algorithms are available: triangular Constant-Q filters and Morlet-shaped filters. It is a logarithmic-resolution time-frequency representation built from an FFT, not a full time-domain wavelet transform.

Signal -> fenêtres -> FFT en dB -> filtres fréquentiels
       -> banc de bandes logarithmiques entre fmin et fmax
       -> seuillage -> correction gamma
       -> interpolation des bandes manquantes
       -> lissage vertical
       -> image temps × bandes par octave

Specific parameters​

ParameterEffect on the visualizationMusical use and trade-offs
Triangular Constant-Q algorithmMore localized bands and sharper renderingReading pitches, partials and attacks; fast and stable
Morlet algorithmSofter, overlapping bandsContinuous textures, glissandi, masses and transitions; slightly softened attacks
Minimum frequency, default 27.5 HzSets the image’s lower bound and total band countIncluding deep bass requires a sufficiently long window; otherwise low bands become sparse or unstable
Maximum frequency, default 7,040 HzSets the image’s upper boundLowering it focuses on pitches/partials; raising it includes noise, hiss and brightness
Bands per octave, default 40, range 6…48Sets vertical densityHigh values: fine intervals and detailed glissandi, but a taller image and a risk of bands without FFT support
Quality, default 12, range 2…32For Morlet, narrows filters around their center frequencyHigh: better partial separation but more discontinuous rendering; low: smoother envelopes but merged neighboring frequencies
Threshold, default 0.02, range 0…1Removes and then rescales weak magnitudesCleans noise and reverberation; too high, it erases weak partials and sound endings
Gamma, default 0.4, range 0.25…4Nonlinear curve after thresholdingAbove 1: weak values become more visible; below 1: greater contrast and darker background
Vertical smoothing, default 1, range 0…4Repeatedly averages neighboring bandsReduces striping; too strong, it merges nearby pitches and partials

Relationships between parameters​

Bands per octave cannot exceed the resolution provided by the FFT. With a short window, greatly increasing this setting adds interpolation or smoothing rather than revealing new information, especially in the bass. For precise low frequencies, increase window size before vertical density.

Quality only affects the Morlet profile. Threshold, gamma and smoothing control readability: they strongly affect appearance and must remain identical in comparisons.

Musical uses​

This view suits pitches over a wide range, partials, glissandi, micro-intervals, resonances and electroacoustic textures. It provides a more musical reading of register than the linear spectrogram while retaining more spectral information than a chromagram.

Stereo balance spectrogram​

Principle and pipeline​

Two synchronized images are computed: mono-mix energy and the left-right position of each spectral bin.

Canal gauche L -> FFT magnitude L --+
                                      -> R / (L + R) par fréquence
Canal droit  R -> FFT magnitude R --+

(L + R) / 2 -> FFT en dB -> couche d'énergie

couche d'énergie + couche de position -> coloration spatiale

The spatial value is approximately 0 on the left, 0.5 in the center and 1 on the right. At least two channels are required; the first two are used.

Specific display parameters​

ParameterEffectMusical use
Left, center and right colorsEncode component pan positionsIdentify lateralized instruments and movement
Full modeCombines the entire balanceOverview of the spectral mix
Left onlyVisually isolates the left regionExamine a lateralized source or event
Center onlyIsolates balanced componentsVoice, bass, kick drum or centrally placed elements
Right onlyVisually isolates the right regionSymmetrical comparison with the left channel

Window size and hop have the same effects as in the linear spectrogram. A large window stabilizes sustained-sound positions; a small window better follows rapid movement and attacks.

Musical uses and limitations​

This view helps analyze spatial orchestration, panning, left-right dialogues, trajectories and the mix’s frequency distribution. It does not by itself measure perceptual width or phase: two decorrelated signals of equal energy may appear centered under this ratio.

MID/SIDE ratio spectrogram​

Principle and pipeline​

The signal is transformed into central and lateral components:

MID  = (L + R) / 2
SIDE = (L - R) / 2

MID  -> FFT magnitude --+
                        -> SIDE / (MID + SIDE) par fréquence
SIDE -> FFT magnitude --+

MID -> FFT en dB -> couche d'énergie

A low value indicates mainly MID content; a high value indicates mainly SIDE content.

Specific display parameters​

ModeWhat it highlightsMusical use
FullMID/SIDE ratio across the spectrumOverview of spectral width
MID onlyContent common to both channelsCentral sources and mono-compatible elements
Balance onlyIntermediate ratio regionMixtures neither strictly central nor strongly lateral
SIDE onlyDifference between channelsStereo reverberation, width, phase effects, doubling and ambience

MID, balance and SIDE colors provide a qualitative legend. They do not replace calibrated correlation or mono-compatibility measurements.

Musical uses and limitations​

This representation helps study mix width by frequency, visually separate center and ambience, identify spatialization effects or assess what may disappear in a mono downmix.

Strong SIDE content may result from spatial placement, reverberation, delay, phase inversion or genuinely different channel content. The image shows the spectral result, not its cause.

How to choose a representation​

Analytical questionSuggested representationStarting settings
Where are attacks and noise masses?Linear spectrogramShort to medium window, medium dynamic range
Which partials and resonances make up the timbre?Linear or logarithmic resolutionMedium to long window, low threshold
How do register, melody or glissandi evolve?Logarithmic or logarithmic resolutionBounds suited to the range, medium window
Which chords or pitch centers dominate?ChromagramCorrect tuning reference; modulation according to the desired prominence of dynamics
Where does timbre or instrumentation change?MFCC or complexityShort hop, identical parameters throughout the piece
Where do spectral bands appear or disappear?ComplexityIncrease or decrease mode, short hop
How is the spectrum distributed left-right?Stereo balanceTwo channels, full mode followed by isolated regions
What belongs to the center or to the width?MID/SIDEFull mode, then MID and SIDE separately

Recommended adjustment method​

  1. Define the musical question before choosing a representation.
  2. Set window size and hop first: they determine the available information.
  3. Choose frequency bounds according to register and material.
  4. Then adjust contrast, threshold, gamma or smoothing to make that information readable.
  5. Keep all parameters unchanged when comparing passages or files.
  6. Check a hypothesis in at least two complementary views and by listening.

A good setting makes the phenomenon under study stable under small adjustments. If a structure disappears after a slight threshold or palette change, interpret it cautiously.

Interpretation pitfalls​

  • A bright line is not necessarily a fundamental: it may be a harmonic or resonance.
  • A dark region does not always mean silence: it may result from threshold, dynamic range or frequency bounds.
  • A more detailed image is not necessarily more accurate; it may be noisier or interpolated.
  • The chromagram deliberately merges octaves.
  • MFCCs have no directly readable frequency axis.
  • Complexity measures a relative difference between frames, not musical complexity in an aesthetic sense.
  • Spatial representations describe spectral energy ratios, not absolute acoustic localization.
  • Every analysis depends on the internal sample rate of 44,100 Hz and the Nyquist limit of 22,050 Hz.
